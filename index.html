Voter FAQ · License Plate Readers

First, undo what we have. Then decide what's next.

I've said this in comments around town, and I want to say it plainly here: I am not for a first-party camera system. My position starts one step earlier — with the system Cabot already has, run by a private, out-of-state company — and with a simple question: do we want to keep it?

If the answer is no, we undo it. Whether Cabot should ever run a system of its own is a different question, for a different day, decided in public. This page walks through both — in that order.

Who's answering: Corey Spangler, in my own words Topic: Flock cameras & automated license plate readers (ALPR) in Cabot
My position in plain English

Step one: end the third-party system. I introduced an ordinance to cut off city funding for third-party ALPR — because the first question isn't "city cameras or company cameras?" It's "do we keep what we already have?"

Step two — later, and only if voters want it: decide whether Cabot should ever run an in-house system, controlled here, subject to public oversight — for example, with no data retention at all. I haven't decided that for you, and I'm not asking you to decide it now.

Two questions. Don't let anyone merge them.

Most of the argument in Cabot right now treats this as one question with two answers: keep Flock, or ban cameras. It isn't. It's two questions, and they have to be answered in order.

1

Do we keep the system we already have?

Today, Cabot's plate readers are owned and run by a third-party vendor on a subscription. Our travel data sits in systems we don't control and can't fully audit.

My answer is no — and my ordinance cuts off the city funding that keeps that arrangement alive.

This is the decision in front of us now
2

Should Cabot ever run its own system?

Only after step one is settled — and only as its own public decision. If we ever have cameras, they should be controlled by the city, subject to public oversight, with rules set by people Cabot voters can hold accountable — for example, no data retention.

I'm not for that system. I'm for asking the question honestly, later, instead of letting a vendor contract answer it for us.

A separate decision, made in the open

Answer question 1 first. Question 2 doesn't get answered by default, by contract renewal, or by me.

What I actually did: an ordinance, not a performance

There's a difference between saying something and doing something, and it matters on this issue.

What I did · Binding

I introduced an ordinance

An ordinance is law. Mine prohibits city funds — sales tax revenue, grants, and asset forfeitures — from being spent on third-party ALPR systems. It honors existing contracts until they expire, protects routine equipment like body cameras and dashcams, and preserves cost-free investigative leads shared by other agencies.

What I abstained from · Symbolic

A voice resolution to "take the cameras down"

A resolution is a statement of opinion. It doesn't bind spending, cancel a contract, or change a single data practice. At the same meeting, I abstained from it — not because I want the cameras to stay, but because I'd rather pass the thing that actually ends the arrangement than perform agreement with the thing that doesn't.

I've been called cautious for that abstention. Fair enough — but caution about symbolism isn't support for Flock. If we want the third-party system gone, the ordinance is the tool that does it, and it's the one I put my name on.

If question 2 ever gets asked, here are my non-negotiables

I'm not proposing an in-house system. But if Cabot ever debates one, I'll only take part in that conversation under public oversight rules like these — the same safeguards written into my plan. A license plate reader should read plates. Nothing more.

30-day purge — or no retention at all

Non-evidentiary scans are deleted after 30 days, with certificates proving the purge. And "no data retention" is squarely on the table for any in-house design.

No facial recognition

Banned outright — along with audio recording. Reading a plate is not a license to identify a face or listen in.

No predictive policing

No algorithms deciding who looks suspicious or where police should watch. That's a judgment call for accountable humans, not software.

No wireless tracking

No Bluetooth or Wi-Fi MAC harvesting, no tracking phones or devices as they move through town. Plates only — and only under the rules above.

Public reporting, on the record

Monthly statistics in the council agenda packet and a public report every six months: plates scanned, alert hits by category, verified hits vs. false positives, and outside searches. If the numbers look bad, you'll see it.

Independent audits

Annual independent security audits and penetration testing, with the results public. Trust isn't the oversight plan — verification is.

Questions voters ask me

Straight answers, in the order voters usually ask them.

What are Flock cameras, and what's happening with them in Cabot right now?

They're automated license plate readers (ALPR) — cameras that scan passing plates and check them against hotlists — run by Flock Safety, a private, out-of-state company, under a subscription contract with the city.

Right now, Cabot's cameras are covered and suspended while the city decides their future. That pause is exactly why this conversation matters: what happens next should be a deliberate public decision, not a contract quietly renewing itself.

So what is your position, in one sentence?

Undo the third-party system first; decide later — separately, and in public — whether Cabot should ever run a system of its own.

That's it. Anyone who tells you my position is "Corey wants the city to build its own camera network" is skipping step one and misquoting step two.

Are you for an in-house, first-party system?

No. I am not for a first-party system, and I want to correct that wherever it's been said about me.

What I've said — a few times, in comments — is narrower: if we are going to have plate readers at all, they should be controlled by the city and subject to public oversight, not run by a third-party vendor. That's a principle about control and accountability, not a proposal to build something.

My actual proposal is step one: undo what we have. The in-house question only comes after that, as its own decision — and "no system at all" is a legitimate answer to it.

Why start with "do we keep what we already have?"

Because it's the question where Cabot can actually agree. 100% of the anti-Flock side says no — they don't want the current system kept. And if enough pro-Flock voters agree that this shouldn't be done by a third party, then we have a consensus big enough to act on.

Starting anywhere else — arguing about hypothetical future systems — divides people who actually agree about the thing in front of us. Let's start where the agreement is, undo the current arrangement, and then have the next debate with a clean slate.

Did you vote to get rid of the cameras? Why did you abstain?

I introduced an ordinance to prohibit city funds from being spent on third-party ALPR systems. An ordinance is binding law — it's the mechanism that actually ends the funding and the arrangement.

At the same meeting, I abstained from a voice resolution calling to take the cameras down for good. A resolution is a statement; it binds nothing and cancels nothing. I won't pretend a symbolic vote is a solution when I have a real one on the table. Preferring the ordinance over the resolution isn't hesitation about the goal — it's insistence on the tool that reaches it.

What exactly does your ordinance do?
  • Cuts off the money: no city funds — sales tax revenue, grants, or asset forfeitures — may be spent on third-party ALPR subscriptions.
  • Honors existing contracts until they expire. We end this the lawful way, not the lawsuit way.
  • Protects normal police equipment: body cameras, dashcams, facility cameras, and traffic systems are not touched.
  • Preserves free investigative leads shared by other agencies at no cost to Cabot.

It does not ban police from doing police work. It ends a vendor arrangement — which is the thing voters are actually arguing about.

Does ending the third-party system make Cabot less safe?

That's the framing the vendor benefits from, and I'd ask you to notice it. The question in front of us isn't "safety or no safety." It's whether a private company should own the tool, hold the data, and bill us forever for it.

Officers keep their body cameras, dashcams, radios, and the free leads other agencies share. What ends is the subscription and the data pipeline running through a third party. And if Cabot later decides it wants a tool of its own, that's question two — debated on its merits, in public, with oversight attached from day one.

What stops a wrongful stop based on a bad camera read?

Under the oversight rules in my plan, no stop happens on a camera read alone. Three steps are required first:

  • Visual plate verification — a human confirms the plate actually matches.
  • Hotlist confirmation through dispatch and state/federal databases.
  • Occupant identification before any stop proceeds.

A camera flag is a lead to check, not a conclusion. Cabot has seen what happens when that distinction gets blurred, and the rules should make it impossible to blur.

Who can see my travel data — and for how long?

Under the current third-party arrangement, honestly, Cabot can't fully answer that — and that is itself the problem. Data flows into lateral sharing pools and national databases we don't monitor.

Under my plan, that ends. Non-evidentiary scans are purged after 30 days, with certificates proving the purge. The lateral sharing pools end too: no more sending Cabot residents' travel data to unmonitored databases, except through a formal bilateral compact or an exigent, case-by-case transfer. Public reports show total scans, hits, false positives, and every external search — so "who looked, and why" is a matter of record.

If Cabot ever did build its own system, what would it look like?

To be clear again: I'm not proposing one, and it would only ever happen as a separate public decision after the third-party system is undone. But voters deserve to know the shape of that debate.

Any in-house system I'd take part in would be owned by Cabot, hosted in Cabot, and governed by people Cabot voters elect — subject to public oversight, with independent audits, the 30-day purge (or no data retention at all, which is absolutely on the table), and hard bans on facial recognition, predictive policing, and wireless tracking. No subscription, no vendor lock-in, no data leaving town by default.

And I won't promise it would be built immediately, cheaply, or at all. That's what "a separate decision" means.

Why not just keep Flock? It works, doesn't it?

Because "it works" skips every question that matters: it works for whom, at what cost, holding whose data, under whose control?

Keeping it means a permanent drain of taxpayer dollars to a vendor we don't control, for data practices we can't audit, renewed by inertia instead of by a vote. Fiscal responsibility, local control, and privacy aren't three different positions here — they're the same position. Undoing the third-party system is where all three point.

What happens next?

Question one gets decided the way it should be: in the open, by elected officials voting on a binding ordinance — the one I've introduced — informed by exactly this conversation with voters.

Then, and only then, question two gets its own honest debate: should Cabot run anything of its own, under what oversight, with what retention rules — including the option of nothing at all. I'll take part in that debate as your representative. I won't pre-decide it for you.

Election Day is November 3, 2026. However you vote, I'd rather you vote knowing exactly where I stand — including the parts that are more complicated than a slogan.
